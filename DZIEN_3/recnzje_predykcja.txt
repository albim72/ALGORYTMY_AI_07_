Robert Eggers’s Nosferatu is a hypnotic horror film that poisons the imagination rather than relying on simple shocks. The director builds the atmosphere patiently, allowing dread to spread through each scene like darkness entering an abandoned house. The cinematography is stunning, filled with painterly compositions, dramatic shadows, and an almost monochromatic palette. Count Orlok is not presented as an elegant seducer, but as an ancient and repulsive force that has survived beyond the limits of human time. The extraordinary makeup and physical performance make his presence fascinating, terrifying, and deeply uncomfortable. Lily-Rose Depp delivers an intense portrayal of a woman torn between desire, illness, destiny, and supernatural terror. The music and sound design maintain an oppressive tension even when very little appears to be happening on screen. The film skillfully combines Gothic romance, folklore, physical horror, and the destructive power of obsession. Eggers does not merely imitate the classic story, but reshapes it into something personal, severe, and visually unforgettable. Nosferatu is a beautiful, suffocating, and disturbing film that continues to haunt the viewer long after the final image disappears.


Robert Eggers’s Nosferatu is visually impressive, but surprisingly cold and emotionally distant. Its slow pace does not always create suspense and often turns simple scenes into exhausting stretches of silence and repetition. The director is so focused on the beauty of each frame that the characters sometimes resemble carefully arranged museum objects rather than living people. Count Orlok is certainly grotesque, but his appearance does not consistently produce the terror the filmmakers seem to intend. Several scenes are so exaggerated and theatrical that they risk provoking laughter instead of fear. Lily-Rose Depp performs with great intensity, although her acting occasionally depends too heavily on trembling, convulsions, and repeated expressions of distress. The dialogue sounds solemn and poetic, but it frequently feels artificial and detached from natural human speech. The film introduces numerous erotic, religious, and psychological symbols without developing all of them in a convincing way. Viewers expecting a dynamic horror story may find the production pretentious, monotonous, and more interested in style than genuine terror. In the end, Nosferatu is technically accomplished, but beneath its beautifully sculpted surface there is surprisingly little emotional blood.
